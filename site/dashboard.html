General practice in England · June–August 2026

Fewer GPs, more same-day care, where need is greatest

Practices serving England's most deprived communities have fewer GPs per patient, yet they deliver as much same-day care as anywhere else. They manage it by asking each GP to do more. This is what 5,855 practices' own appointment records show.

1 · Need

More illness, fewer doctors

Compared with practices in the least deprived fifth of England, practices in the most deprived fifth have twice the smoking, three-quarters more COPD and nearly two-thirds more serious mental illness.

They also have 16% fewer qualified GPs per patient and train fewer new GPs. Their patients miss nearly twice as many appointments, and each of those is a gap to chase.

Their lists are younger (13% aged 65+ against 23%), so conditions of older age such as atrial fibrillation are less common. The illness that tracks deprivation is the kind that arrives earlier in life.

2 · Workload

The gap is filled by GPs working harder

Same-day access per patient is roughly flat across deprivation: about 114 same-day GP appointments per 1,000 patients a month in the least deprived fifth and 118 in the most.

With fewer GPs, that means each GP in the most deprived practices delivers 229 same-day appointments a month, against 188. This holds within the same ICB too. Deprived practices have fewer GPs than better-off neighbours under the same commissioner, so the gap is not just a north–south story.

3 · The lever

More GPs, more same-day appointments

Practices with the most GPs per patient deliver 32% more same-day GP appointments per patient than those with the fewest.

The link survives adjustment for deprivation, age, sex, smoking, long-term conditions and the practice's nurses, other clinicians and admin staff.

≈76 more a monthsame-day GP appointments for a typical 10,000-patient practice with one more full-time GP (95% CI 59–93).

4 · The pipeline

Fewest new GPs trained where they're needed most

66% of practices in the least deprived fifth had GPs in training in their workforce return. In the most deprived fifth it was 48%.

Training places bring new doctors into an area, and the communities with the fewest GPs get the smallest share of them.

What would help

Four asks

Restore core funding, weighted to need

Bring core contract funding back to its 2008/09 real-terms level (£… a year, about … salaried GPs). Weight it towards deprivation and early-onset illness: the most deprived fifth of practices alone needs about 650 more GPs to match the staffing of the least deprived.

Core + QOF per person: £… vs £… if it had kept pace.

Grow GP training in deprived areas

Support practices in deprived areas to become training practices: supervision time, premises and educator funding. That way new GPs start their careers where the gap is widest.

Practices training GPs: 48% vs 66%.

Judge access against capacity

Same-day access per patient is already level across deprivation. Access targets and league tables should account for staffing and need, so practices doing the most with the least are not judged as failing.

Same-day GP appointments per GP: 229 vs 188 a month.

Resource the work behind missed appointments

Missed appointments in deprived areas mean chasing, rebooking and safeguarding. Fund outreach, flexible booking and reminders where DNA rates are highest, rather than counting DNAs against the practice.

DNAs per 1,000 patients a month: 27 vs 14.

How strong is this?

The evidence, and its limits

The strongest single association in this data is between missed appointments and a practice's population. DNA rates fall steeply as the share of older patients rises (ρ = −0.50) and climb with deprivation (ρ = 0.47).

For same-day access itself, GP staffing is the strongest factor, but it is modest (ρ ≈ 0.17). Everything measured here together explains only about 5% of the difference between practices in same-day GP appointments. The rest comes from how each practice organises, triages and records its work, which the national data cannot see.

  • These are associations across practices at one point in time. They are consistent with GP numbers driving access, but they do not prove it.
  • Staff employed by PCNs through the ARRS scheme (pharmacists, paramedics, physician associates and others) are not attributed to practices. Deprived areas may have received more of these roles, which would narrow the gap shown here.
  • Appointment recording varies between practices and systems. Practices with list sizes under 1,000, implausible appointment volumes or over 40% unmapped appointments are excluded.
  • Sources: NHS England Appointments in General Practice (June–August 2026), GP Workforce (November 2025), Patients Registered at a GP Practice (July 2026), QOF 2024/25 via Fingertips, IMD 2025.

Definitions and caveats

Access measures

  • Same-day: appointments where the time between booking and appointment is "Same Day". Rates are per 1,000 registered patients per month; the 3-month view is the monthly average.
  • Urgent-type: national categories General Consultation Acute, Clinical Triage, Unplanned Clinical Activity and Walk-in. Urgent same-day is those categories booked and delivered on the same day.
  • All appointment statuses are counted (attended, DNA and unknown), matching how NHS Digital reports appointment volumes. DNA rate is DNAs as a share of all appointments.

Factors

    Things to keep in mind

    • The appointments data is management information from practice systems. Recording of booking dates, staff types and categories varies between practices; 12% of appointments nationally sit in unmapped categories, which mostly affects the urgent-type measures.
    • Denominators use the December 2025 registered list. Practices that merged or opened since may show inflated or missing rates.
    • August is a holiday month with one bank holiday, so its volumes run about 15% below June and July.
    • Correlation is not causation. Disease prevalences are strongly linked to age and to each other, so adjusted coefficients for related factors (e.g. hypertension and CHD) share the same signal and can flip sign.
    • Age and sex: this build has a proxy for age (share of patients under 17). For full age bands, % male and smoking, load the NHS Digital quinary-age file and a QOF smoking extract above.
    • Appointments per GP FTE share a denominator with the staffing measures, so practices with few GPs per patient will mechanically show more appointments per GP. Read those two together, not as cause and effect.
    • Workforce: practice-level returns from the NHS Digital GP workforce collection, a single month's snapshot. Staff employed by PCNs (ARRS roles such as clinical pharmacists, paramedics and physician associates) are not attributed to practices, so practices in PCNs that deliver same-day hubs may look under-staffed for the appointments they record. Practices that did not submit a full return are estimated or missing.
