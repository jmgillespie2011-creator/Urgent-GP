General practice in England · June–August 2026

Fewer GPs where need is greatest

Practices serving England's most deprived communities have fewer GPs per patient, yet provide as much same-day care as other practices. Each GP therefore carries more of the workload. Core funding is now 19% below its 2008/09 value in real terms.

Need

Higher need, fewer GPs

Compared with practices in the least deprived fifth of England, the most deprived fifth have twice the smoking, three-quarters more COPD and nearly two-thirds more serious mental illness.

They also have 16% fewer GPs per patient, and fewer of them train new GPs. Their patients also miss nearly twice as many appointments.

Their lists are younger (13% aged 65+ against 23%), so conditions of older age such as atrial fibrillation are less common. The conditions that are more common in deprived areas tend to develop at younger ages.

Workload

Each GP provides more same-day care

Same-day access per patient is similar across all levels of deprivation: about 114 same-day GP appointments per 1,000 patients a month in the least deprived fifth and 118 in the most.

With fewer GPs, each GP in the most deprived practices delivers 229 same-day appointments a month, against 188. The same pattern is seen between practices within the same ICB, so it is not explained by region.

Funding

Core funding is worth 19% less than in 2008/09

In 2008/09 practices received £99.13 per person in core contract and QOF funding. Had that kept pace with inflation it would be £152.94 in 2023/24. Practices received £123.70.

Across England that is £1.69bn a year, enough for about 15,500 salaried GPs at £109,658 each including employer costs.

For comparison, the BMA called for £152.50 per weighted patient in 2025/26, close to the inflation-adjusted figure above.

Staffing and access

More GPs per patient, more same-day appointments

Practices with the most GPs per patient deliver 32% more same-day GP appointments than those with the fewest. The association remains after adjusting for deprivation, age, sex, smoking, long-term conditions and other practice staff.

≈76 a monthmore same-day GP appointments for each extra full-time GP (95% CI 59–93).

Restoring core funding to its 2008/09 value would pay for about 15,500 GPs. Based on this association, that could mean about 1.17 million more same-day GP appointments every month, 15% more than practices deliver now.

Recommendations

Restore core funding, weighted to need

Bring core contract funding back to its 2008/09 real-terms value, about £1.69bn a year, and weight it towards deprivation and early-onset illness.

Per person: £123.70 now vs £152.94 if it had kept pace.

Expand GP training in deprived areas

Fund supervision time, premises and educators so that more practices in deprived areas can train GPs.

Practices training GPs: 48% vs 66%, most vs least deprived.

Judge access against capacity

Access targets and league tables should account for staffing and need, so that practices with fewer GPs and higher need are assessed fairly.

Same-day GP appointments per GP: 229 vs 188 a month.

Fund work to reduce missed appointments

Fund outreach, flexible booking and reminders in practices with the highest rates of missed appointments.

Missed appointments per 1,000 patients a month: 27 vs 14.

Methods and limitations

Strength of the evidence

The strongest association in the data is between missed appointments and a practice's population. DNA rates fall as the share of older patients rises (rank correlation −0.50) and rise with deprivation (rank correlation 0.47).

For same-day access, GP staffing has the strongest association, but it is modest (rank correlation about 0.17). Deprivation, age, sex, smoking, long-term conditions and staffing together explain about 5% of the difference between practices. The remaining variation reflects how practices organise, triage and record appointments, which national data does not capture.

The adjusted estimate (76 more same-day GP appointments a month per extra full-time GP, 95% CI 59–93) comes from one linear model across 5,413 practices. It includes deprivation, age 65+, age 0–14, % male, smoking, long-term condition burden, and nurse, other clinical and admin staffing. Its confidence interval allows for practices in the same ICB being alike.

A rank correlation runs from −1 to +1. Zero means no association. It measures the strength of an association, not statistical significance, and is not a p-value. With nearly 6,000 practices, even weak associations are statistically significant, so the size of the correlation is the relevant measure.

  • These are associations across practices at one point in time. They are consistent with GP numbers driving access, but they do not prove it.
  • Staff employed by PCNs through the ARRS scheme (pharmacists, paramedics, physician associates and others) are not attributed to practices. Deprived areas may have received more of them, which would narrow the staffing gap.
  • Appointment recording varies between practices. Practices with fewer than 1,000 patients, implausible volumes or over 40% unmapped appointments are left out of charts and totals.
Funding shortfall calculation
  • 2008/09 England core contract + QOF funding: Global Sum + MPIG, PMS baseline, APMS/PCTMS core and QOF, £5,136m (NHS Digital, Investment in General Practice 2008/09–2012/13). QOF is included because QOF points were later moved into the global sum. The 2023/24 figure counts the same items (NHS Payments to General Practice).
  • Uprated by ONS CPI (×1.543, financial-year averages) and England's population (×1.119), then compared with what was actually paid.
  • Seniority pay was also later folded into the global sum but cannot be separated in 2008/09. It is left out, which makes the estimated shortfall smaller than it is.
  • Funding for PCN-employed staff (ARRS), premises, IT and dispensing sits outside the core contract and is not counted.
  • Each practice's share is the national gap split by weighted patients, the same needs weighting the global sum uses.
  • Salaried GP cost: midpoint of the BMA model contract range plus employer NI and the employer pension contribution practices pay.
  • That many GPs would mean about 2.5 more per 10,000 patients, which takes the typical practice to the top of today's staffing range. The same-day figure is an extrapolation at the edge of the data, and it assumes the GPs could be recruited.
Definitions and data sources
  • Same-day: booked and delivered on the same day. Urgent same-day: national categories General Consultation Acute, Clinical Triage, Unplanned Clinical Activity and Walk-in, booked the same day. Rates are per 1,000 registered patients per month, averaged over June–August 2026.
  • Deprivation fifths: practices ranked by IMD 2025 practice score and split into five equal groups.
  • GPs per 10,000 patients: qualified GPs (excluding GPs in training) in full-time equivalents, November 2025.
  • Sources: NHS England Appointments in General Practice (June–August 2026), GP Workforce (November 2025), Patients Registered at a GP Practice (July 2026), QOF 2024/25 via Fingertips, IMD 2025, NHS Payments to General Practice (2023/24), Investment in General Practice (2008/09), ONS CPI and population estimates.
